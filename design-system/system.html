Scorecard · Design System

The signal in the noise — encoded.

A reference for everyone shipping Scorecard surfaces. Logo, color, type, components, and motifs — all built around a single visual idea: most prospects are noise, a few are the signal.

Three overlapping curves form a distribution silhouette — two muted bumps for the noise, one tall colored bump for the signal. The wordmark sits at the same baseline as the curves' base.

Clearspace
Maintain clearspace equal to the height of the wordmark's cap-height on all sides.
Don't
  • Don't recolor the curves outside the brand palette
  • Don't outline, drop-shadow, or emboss the mark
  • Don't separate the curves from the wordmark in lockup contexts
  • Don't place on busy or saturated photography

06 · Voice

How Scorecard sounds.

Direct, confident, lightly skeptical of marketing-speak. Plainspoken operator energy. We sell by showing, not telling — "we'd rather show the work than pitch it."

Tone
Direct. Confident. Plainspoken. Slight wry undertone when alternatives are dressed up — "lists go stale on day one," "tools fight each other for the same job." Comfortable saying what we are not: not a list provider, not a tool, not another agency.
Personality
Operator-grade. Specific. Plainspoken. Skeptical-of-hype. Confidently picky. We don't take every customer; we say so on the page.
Style rules
Short sentences
Two-clause max. Period beats em dash when the rhythm permits. Vary length — but lean tight.
Specific numbers
"30–150 won deals," "500–2,000 names," "Week 1–2." Numbers earn their place. Round numbers are usually a tell that we're guessing.
Active voice
"Your reps close deals." Not "deals are closed by your reps." Subject does the work.
Concrete over abstract
"The day disappears into list-building" beats "improving sales productivity." Imagery the buyer recognizes from their own week.
No exclamation. No emoji.
No "!". No "🚀" / "✅". The work speaks; we don't decorate it.
Em dashes earn their use
Em dashes wrap a real parenthetical or sharpen a contrast. Not a tic. A comma or a period is usually fine.
Voice rules of thumb
Lead with the customer's problem in their language
Open with what their week looks like. "Reps spend half their week building the list and the other half pretending the list is good." If the buyer doesn't recognize their own day, the page won't earn the read.
Quantify wherever possible
Deals, weeks, dollars, prospects. "First batch in week 1–2, monthly delivery from week 4." Specific commitments build trust where logos and testimonials would.
Show, don't pitch
The intro call walks through sample names on their data. Not slides. Not a demo loop. The product is most credible when the buyer sees the work.
Earn the claim
Every differentiator pairs with the operating principle behind it. Not "best-in-class scoring" — "every score is explainable, line by line."
Define what we're not, crisply
"Not a list provider. Not a tool. Not another agency." Negation sharpens the positive — a dropped category is the fastest way to position the next sentence.
Outcome over mechanism
"More paying customers" leads. "Scoring rubric" follows only when the buyer needs the why. Mechanism words (rubric, motion, calibrate) sit inside the explanation, never in the headline.
Words we use
Customers, deals, reps, names, queue, batch, ICP, tier, week, month. Buyer vocabulary. Sales-team vernacular. Specific enough to argue with.
Words we avoid
Disrupt, revolutionize, 10x. Empty intensifiers.
Empower, unlock, leverage, synergize. Generic SaaS verbs.
Per-lead pricing, cost per lead. Wrong frame — we sell a managed service, not a list.
Model, ML, algorithm, machine learning. Buyer is a sales team, not an engineer. Use "scoring," "rubric," or just "the work."
