# Scorecard website brief

**Date:** July 16, 2026
**Status:** Strategic direction for the next homepage and supporting site
**Primary conversion:** A qualified conversation that begins with the Growth Planning Intensive

## 1. Executive direction

Scorecard should not present itself as account-scoring software, a collection of GTM services, or another dashboard. It should present itself as a **managed growth system**: Scorecard combines strategy, proprietary software, and embedded operators to turn a revenue goal into a plan the company can run—and then implements it in the CRM and operates the reporting, sourcing, scoring, and adaptation needed to keep it current.

The website’s central promise is:

> **More revenue. Fewer surprises.**

The emotional transformation is from reactive, fragmented growth to controlled, ambitious momentum. Before Scorecard, each week is another outcome to explain. With Scorecard, each week becomes another deliberate step toward the goal.

The website should make that transformation feel obvious before it explains the machinery behind it.

## 2. What Scorecard is

### Category

**A managed growth system for revenue teams.**

This category is intentionally broader than RevOps consulting, account scoring, outbound services, or planning software. It describes the combination customers actually buy:

- A credible full-funnel plan behind the revenue target
- Proprietary software that connects the plan to operating evidence
- Forward-deployed GTM operators who run the recurring work
- Managed services for CRM implementation, reporting, market intelligence, sourcing, scoring, and plan adaptation
- A weekly management loop that shows what changed, what matters now, and what to do next

An internal shorthand is “the Palantir model for revenue”: strategic advisory plus forward-deployed GTM engineering plus proprietary software. That analogy is useful for product and brand decisions, but it does not need to appear in customer-facing copy.

### The simplest value proposition

Scorecard helps a company make more revenue with fewer surprises by connecting five things that are usually disconnected:

1. **Plan:** Turn the revenue goal into explicit market, funnel, capacity, and investment assumptions.
2. **Measure:** Connect the plan to CRM and operating data so progress is visible every week.
3. **Prioritize:** Source and score the accounts most likely to create leverage for the sales team.
4. **Implement:** Put the plan into the CRM and operating cadence the team already uses.
5. **Adapt:** Use weekly intelligence to fix constraints and compound what works.

### What Scorecard is not

- Not another CRM
- Not a passive analytics dashboard
- Not a strategy deck that expires after the workshop
- Not an outbound agency optimizing its own activity metrics
- Not a black-box lead score
- Not software the customer must learn and operate alone

## 3. Audience and buying situation

### Primary buyers

- CEOs and founders accountable for an ambitious revenue target
- Revenue leaders accountable for converting that target into an operating plan
- Growth-stage and middle-market companies with a real sales team, a CRM, and enough complexity that disconnected owners and systems have become a constraint

### Secondary stakeholders

- Sales leaders who need better territories, accounts, capacity decisions, and seller leverage
- Marketing and growth leaders who need a shared definition of the market and funnel
- RevOps leaders who need a plan against which CRM activity can be interpreted
- Boards and investors who need a credible model, not just a top-line number

### The moment they become receptive

The buyer usually knows the revenue target but cannot answer, with confidence:

- Which assumptions must be true to reach it?
- Which growth constraint matters most right now?
- Are we short on market, conversion, pipeline, capacity, or execution?
- Are teams and vendors working on the same constraint?
- Why did performance change this week?
- What should we fund, fix, or stop next?

They are not primarily shopping for “scoring.” They are trying to regain control of growth without adding another disconnected tool or vendor.

## 4. The customer transformation

### Before Scorecard

- The board target lives in one file; the assumptions live elsewhere.
- Market, funnel, capacity, and investment are modeled by different owners.
- The CRM records activity but does not show whether the plan is working.
- Teams and vendors optimize their own metrics rather than the shared growth constraint.
- Salespeople react to whatever appears urgent and spend too much time finding the next account.
- Weekly reviews explain the past but do not reliably sequence the next action.
- Results fluctuate, and leadership cannot tell whether a good week is momentum or noise.

### After Scorecard

- One explicit model connects the revenue goal to the market, funnel, capacity, and investment required.
- CRM evidence is read against the plan rather than mistaken for the plan.
- The market is sourced, scored, and ranked with evidence behind each recommendation.
- Operators run the reporting and execution workstreams the plan requires.
- Sales spends more time in the right conversations.
- The weekly review reveals the constraint, recommends the next action, and updates the forecast.
- Each week builds on the last: measured, corrected, and still aimed at an ambitious outcome.

### Emotional promise

The customer should leave the homepage feeling:

- **Control:** We can see the system behind the number.
- **Confidence:** The plan has explicit assumptions and accountable owners.
- **Leverage:** Our sellers spend more time selling, supported by a system around them.
- **Momentum:** Each week creates evidence and progress instead of another reset.

“Predictable revenue” is a useful aspiration, but the site should not imply certainty. Prefer **more predictable growth**, **fewer surprises**, **a plan your team can run**, and **every week builds**.

## 6. Homepage narrative

The homepage should follow the emotional logic learned from the strongest reference work: recognize the burden, perform the transformation, then substantiate the new state. It should not begin with a taxonomy of services.

### 1. Header

**Navigation:** Solutions ▾, Pricing, About
**CTA:** Define your growth plan

The Solutions menu can contain:

- Growth planning
- CRM implementation
- Weekly growth intelligence
- Market intelligence, sourcing, and scoring
- Plan adaptation

### 2. Hero: the promised outcome

Lead with “More revenue. Fewer surprises.” and the managed-system explanation. The hero should feel consequential, calm, and executive.

The visual is an emotional sales asset, not a product explainer. It should show reactive weekly outcomes becoming cumulative, ambitious progress. Do not put a simplified dashboard at the center of the hero.

### 3. Problem recognition: why growth stays reactive

**Recommended headline:** What has to change to hit your revenue goals?

Show the fragmentation leaders already recognize: the target, market, funnel, CRM, capacity plan, vendors, and weekly report all exist, but they do not operate as one system. A concise before/after comparison is appropriate here.

The purpose of this section is recognition, not education. The buyer should think: “That is exactly why the number feels harder to manage than it should.”

### 4. The transformation: every week builds

Reveal the simple operating loop beneath the outcome:

**Plan → Implement → Measure → Prioritize → Adapt**

Show how these actions convert disconnected weekly effort into compounding progress. Keep the explanation subordinate to the emotional before/after.

Suggested copy:

> Before Scorecard, every week is another result to explain. With Scorecard, every week becomes another step toward the goal.

### 5. What leadership receives

**Recommended headline:** What leadership receives—not another login.

Use three tangible outputs:

1. **The plan behind the number** — the full-funnel model and its assumptions
2. **The work the plan requires** — CRM implementation, account priorities, and reporting
3. **The weekly management read** — plan versus actual, forecast, exceptions, and recommended actions

These should be shown with real or faithfully anonymized artifacts, not generic feature cards.

### 6. Solutions: the system’s workstreams

Present capabilities as coordinated parts of one growth system rather than unrelated services:

- **Growth planning:** Define the market, funnel, capacity, and investment required.
- **Reporting and intelligence:** Connect the plan to CRM evidence and produce the weekly operating read.
- **Sourcing and scoring:** Find, enrich, rank, and verify the accounts most likely to create leverage.
- **Plan adaptation:** Update the forecast, priorities, and next actions as weekly evidence changes.

The key visual idea is convergence: separate workstreams feed one rising revenue motion. This is the appropriate place for the “One Revenue Motion” animation study if the hero remains focused on emotional outcome.

### 7. The operating model

**Recommended headline:** Your team keeps selling. Scorecard runs the system.

Use three steps:

1. **Build it together** — begin with the Growth Planning Intensive.
2. **We operate it** — Scorecard’s GTM operators and software run the recurring work.
3. **You lead from it** — leadership reviews decisions, exceptions, priorities, and the weekly forecast.

Make responsibility visible. The operator is part of the product, not an implementation footnote.

### 8. How it fits

Reduce adoption anxiety:

- The CRM stays the source of operating truth.
- Reps do not need another daily workspace.
- The model shows its assumptions and evidence.
- A named operator owns the recurring output.
- Services follow the plan and change when the constraint changes.

### 9. Proof

Proof is the site’s most important unresolved requirement. It should establish causal credibility, not merely decorate the claims.

The ideal proof sequence is:

**Starting condition → model insight → operated service → management action → measured result**

Prioritize:

- An anonymized real full-funnel growth model
- A real weekly intelligence report and the decision it changed
- A real sourcing/scoring output with evidence and CRM delivery
- A case story showing the sequence from insight to action to result
- Named operator experience and a clear operating cadence
- Verified integration and data-quality claims

Do not invent customer logos, revenue lifts, activity counts, or interface states. When causal proof is incomplete, promise traceability: the site can show how a recommendation follows from evidence and assumptions without claiming Scorecard alone caused the outcome.

### 10. Entry offer and pricing

The Growth Planning Intensive is the natural entry point because it converts the buyer’s number into an explicit operating plan and exposes the highest-leverage work.

**Recommended headline:** Bring the number. Leave with the system behind it.

Explain what the buyer brings, what Scorecard builds, what decisions become possible, how long the engagement takes, and what happens next. If prices can be made public, publish a clear starting price or engagement structure. If not, the Pricing page should still explain how engagements are scoped and what drives cost.

### 11. FAQ and final CTA

Answer the questions most likely to block a qualified conversation:

- Is Scorecard software or a service?
- Does Scorecard replace our CRM or RevOps team?
- What does the Growth Planning Intensive include?
- Which workstreams can Scorecard operate?
- How quickly does the weekly operating loop begin?
- How are accounts scored and verified?
- What does our team need to own?

Close with the same promise and CTA used in the hero.

## 7. Hero animation brief

### Concept: Every Week Builds

The animation should transform **spikes into stairs**.

#### Before

A series of weekly outcome marks rise, fall, and restart. Some weeks are good, some are poor, but there is no visible system connecting them. The motion feels reactive and slightly unstable without becoming chaotic.

#### Intervention

A restrained operating rail appears beneath the outcomes:

**Plan · Implement · Measure · Prioritize · Adapt**

Each action briefly activates. The existing weekly marks do not disappear; they reorganize. This is important: Scorecard creates leverage from the company’s people, CRM, market, and activity rather than pretending to replace them.

#### After

The weekly units lock into an ascending staircase toward a higher goal. The path retains slight variation so it communicates managed ambition rather than guaranteed linear growth. A subtle goal line remains ahead, keeping the outcome aspirational.

#### Suggested loop

- **0–2 seconds:** Reactive weekly outcomes appear and fluctuate.
- **2–4 seconds:** Plan, Implement, Measure, Prioritize, and Adapt activate in sequence.
- **4–7 seconds:** The same units align into an ascending path.
- **7–9 seconds:** Hold on the ambitious after-state, then dissolve gently into the next loop.

#### Visual treatment

- Black-and-white wireframe logic first; color only after the motion reads clearly
- Dark ink mosaic field continuous across the hero and visual, with no large gap between them
- Fine light lines and pale blocks; semantic color appears sparingly during intervention
- No dashboard chrome, floating document collage, or miniature software UI
- No bounce, spring, parallax, celebratory confetti, or exaggerated acceleration
- Respect reduced-motion settings with a static before/after composition

### Supporting animation: One Revenue Motion

Use later on the page to explain the actions without overloading the hero. Five initially separate lanes—Plan, Implement, Measure, Prioritize, Adapt—converge into one rising revenue trajectory. This is the simplest way to show that Scorecard’s services are coordinated by the plan.

The sourcing/scoring “Right Conversations” filter can be used within its solution section: a broad account field narrows to a small evidence-backed priority set, creating more selling time. It is valuable but too narrow to represent the whole company in the hero.

## 8. Visual design direction

Use the July 14 Scorecard system as the source of visual truth.

### Character

Quiet, technical, and confident—closer to a modernist annual report or decision system than a conventional SaaS landing page.

### Core rules

- Inter throughout; do not introduce an editorial serif without an explicit brand decision
- Ink `#03153A` as the dominant dark tone
- Background `#FAFBFC` and white for light sections
- Gold `#FCAF0F`, green `#A8D862`, blue `#649BE4`, and purple `#B493EA` used semantically, not decoratively
- Eight-pixel spacing rhythm
- Centered content up to 1280px, with 48px desktop gutters and 24px mobile gutters
- Hairline borders, restrained radii, and one dominant shadow moment at most
- Tabular numerals for goals, forecasts, and operating metrics
- Almost no marketing icons
- No stock photography or generic AI imagery

### The mosaic

The mosaic is Scorecard’s most ownable graphic language. In the hero, use the dark version with softly animated light passing through the cells. It can imply fragmented operating evidence becoming a visible pattern without turning into a literal data visualization.

The mosaic should support hierarchy, not become wallpaper. Use high contrast around the promise and animation, then let the page move into calm light sections for explanation and proof.

### Product imagery

When the page shows product or service artifacts, show real work at a legible scale:

- A model and its assumptions
- A weekly intelligence read
- An evidence-backed account ranking
- A CRM-delivered action or verified output

Avoid decorative miniature dashboards that cannot be inspected and do not prove a claim.

## 9. Motion principles

- One major animated argument per section
- Motion should reveal cause and effect, not simply add energy
- Reuse the same visual objects before and after so the transformation is felt
- Keep ambient loops slow and restrained
- Preserve an immediate readable state before JavaScript loads
- Ensure every animated story has a strong reduced-motion equivalent
- Treat scroll as pacing, not as a reason for complex choreography

The Verbrio lesson to retain is not “papers become a dashboard.” It is that the visual performs the value proposition. For Scorecard, the performed transformation is **reactive weekly effort becoming cumulative revenue momentum**.

## 11. Conversion strategy

The homepage should optimize for a qualified executive conversation, not free-trial volume.

### Primary journey

Promise → recognition → transformation → tangible outputs → operating model → proof → Growth Planning Intensive

### CTA language

Primary: **Define your growth plan**
Secondary: **See how Scorecard works**

Avoid “Get started,” “Book a demo,” and other generic CTA language when a more specific next step is available.

The form or scheduler should ask only enough to prepare a useful first conversation: company, current revenue/run rate, revenue goal, sales-team size, CRM, and the largest suspected growth constraint.

## 12. Proof and content required before final production

The design can proceed with anonymized placeholders, but the public site should not ship its strongest claims without these inputs:

- One real full-funnel growth model cleared for anonymized use
- One weekly intelligence report cleared for anonymized use
- One sourcing/scoring example with visible evidence and verification
- One case narrative with starting condition, decision, action, and result
- A precise Growth Planning Intensive scope, timeline, and pricing approach
- A current list of systems Scorecard can truthfully integrate with
- Operator bios and the recurring customer cadence
- Clear boundaries around what Scorecard operates versus what the customer owns

## 13. Guardrails

- Do not reduce the company to account scoring. Scoring is one leverage-producing workstream inside the managed system.
- Do not present a feature inventory before the customer understands the outcome.
- Do not use a dashboard as the hero metaphor.
- Do not make the visual more sophisticated than the idea.
- Do not imply a guaranteed straight-line revenue result.
- Do not call the CRM the plan; it supplies evidence against the plan.
- Do not imply that AI replaces accountable operators or customer judgment.
- Do not invent proof, logos, integrations, metrics, or product states.
- Do not make the customer adopt another daily workspace to receive the value.
- Do not let service modules appear to compete for attention; they all follow the shared constraint.

## 14. Decisions this brief supersedes

The earlier scoring-first relaunch direction remains useful as a solution narrative but no longer defines the homepage or company category. Its strongest ideas should be preserved inside the sourcing and scoring story:

- The whole market, not only the easy-to-find slice
- Evidence behind every score
- Human verification before delivery
- Done-for-you operation rather than another login
- More selling time for reps

The current strategic hierarchy is:

1. **Company:** Managed growth system
2. **Promise:** More revenue. Fewer surprises.
3. **Operating logic:** Plan, implement, measure, prioritize, adapt
4. **Delivery model:** Strategy + proprietary software + embedded GTM operators
5. **Service modules:** Planning, CRM implementation, reporting, sourcing, scoring, and plan adaptation

## 15. Definition of success

The website is working when a qualified buyer can answer these questions after one visit:

- What is Scorecard? A managed growth system, operated with us.
- What business outcome does it create? More revenue with fewer surprises.
- How does it work? It connects the plan, CRM implementation, weekly reporting, account priorities, and adaptation in one loop.
- Why is it different? The strategy, software, services, and accountable operator are one system.
- What will I receive? A plan, operated workstreams, and a weekly management read.
- What happens next? We define the growth plan together through the Growth Planning Intensive.

The ideal reaction is not “That looks like interesting software.” It is:

> “This is the system we are missing behind our revenue goal.”
